## 0. Purpose

This framework is for **pure academic research**, especially AI/ML research whose goal is to produce a genuine scientific contribution that can become a defensible conference paper and contribute useful knowledge back to the research community.

It is deliberately separate from challenge/competition and production-engineering workflows.

The framework has two inseparable outputs:

1. **Scientific contribution** — a new, bounded, evidence-supported insight, method, phenomenon, representation, objective, theory, evaluation, dataset, or meaningful negative result.
2. **Researcher development** — stronger understanding, research taste, question formulation, experimental judgment, critical reasoning, debate, writing, and scientific defense.

The process is human-centered. AI and tools amplify the researcher; they do not replace ownership of understanding, judgment, or ideas.

---

## 1. Core Philosophy

Academic research is **not**:

> Survey → Idea → Code → Success → Paper

The canonical shape is:

> Understand ↔ Survey ↔ Synthesize ↔ Question ↔ Idea ↔ Debate ↔ Hypothesis ↔ Experiment ↔ Evidence/Failure ↔ Belief Update ↔ Re-understand

This loop may repeat many times before a contribution crystallizes.

Failure is normal. A failed hypothesis that improves understanding can be more scientifically valuable than a positive benchmark gain with no mechanistic explanation.

The default assumption is never “our method will work.”

The default assumption is:

> **We do not know yet.**

Research exists to reduce that uncertainty honestly.

## 2. Non-Negotiable Invariants

### 2.1 Human understanding is the primary state variable
The researcher must personally become able to explain, reconstruct, critique, connect, generate, and defend the work.

AI may:
- search literature,
- explain difficult concepts,
- compare papers,
- challenge interpretations,
- help organize knowledge,
- assist coding,
- analyze results,
- simulate reviewers.

AI must not become a substitute for the researcher's scientific understanding or judgment.

### 2.2 No knowledge ceiling
If understanding a paper requires unfamiliar:
- mathematics,
- optimization,
- probability,
- geometry,
- systems,
- physics,
- neuroscience,
- statistics,
- domain knowledge,
- broader world knowledge,

then pause and learn it deeply enough to understand the work on its own terms.

Do not simplify around a prerequisite merely to keep moving.

### 2.3 Deep reading is adaptive
Do not read every paper with the same template or depth.

Allocate effort according to where the intellectual contribution actually lives:
- architecture,
- representation,
- objective,
- theory,
- data,
- evaluation,
- experimental evidence,
- negative result.

### 2.4 Reconstruct author intent scientifically
Try to understand:
- what problem pressure the authors responded to,
- what assumptions they inherited,
- why they chose this formulation,
- what trade-offs they accepted,
- which components are essential vs scaffolding.

Always distinguish:
- **explicitly stated by authors**,
- **strongly supported interpretation**,
- **our inference/hypothesis about intent**.

### 2.5 Survey is field reconstruction, not paper collection
The goal is to recover:
- lineage,
- paradigms,
- assumptions,
- mechanisms,
- turning points,
- dead branches,
- disagreements,
- unresolved tensions.

“Read 50 papers” is not a completion condition.

### 2.6 Questions are first-class research objects
Prefer questions that are:
- precise,
- consequential,
- discriminative,
- grounded,
- generative.

Good research often starts from a tension or unexplained observation, not from “I need novelty.”

### 2.7 Do not formalize intuition too early
Allow messy idea incubation:
- sketches,
- analogies,
- counterfactuals,
- discussions,
- thought experiments,
- reading.

Formalize only when the intuition is mature enough to become testable.

### 2.8 Experiments are scientific interrogations
An experiment should answer a question or discriminate explanations.

Before important runs, know:
- what is being asked,
- competing explanations,
- expected outcomes,
- controls,
- confounds,
- what would change belief.

### 2.9 Performance gain is an observation, not an explanation
A higher score does not by itself prove:
- why a method works,
- what mechanism caused the gain,
- that the scientific claim is correct.

### 2.10 Failure must update beliefs
Do not automatically rescue failed hypotheses by:
- tuning,
- adding modules,
- changing backbones,
- extending training.

First classify the failure and decide what it teaches.

### 2.11 Scientific debate is continuous
Debate belongs throughout the project:
- paper debate,
- proposal attack,
- alternative explanations,
- reviewer simulation,
- rebuttal drills,
- oral/whiteboard defense.

### 2.12 Novelty is not contribution
A new combination of modules may be novel but scientifically weak.

A contribution should answer:

> **What does the field know, understand, or become able to do after this work that it did not before?**

### 2.13 Claim strength must match evidence strength
Do not overclaim.

Distinguish:
- observation,
- pattern,
- finding,
- hypothesis,
- supported mechanism,
- causal claim,
- speculation.

### 2.14 Tools are instruments, not the research
Use spreadsheets, slides, diagrams, notebooks, reference managers, scripts, trackers, etc. only when they:
- reduce mechanical burden,
- expose structure,
- improve evidence analysis,
- improve communication.

Use the lightest useful tool.

Avoid artifact bureaucracy.

### 2.15 Every major next step must be justified
The key decision question is:

> **Why is this the most scientifically informative next action?**

Not:

> “What comes next in the workflow?”

---


### 2.16 High Scientific Ambition / No Artificial Ceiling

Default ambition for pure academic research:

> **Aim for the strongest scientifically defensible contribution the project can support.**

Do not optimize toward:
- the minimum publishable unit,
- an artificially small novelty claim,
- an easier low-tier venue merely for acceptance,
- premature stopping once the work is "good enough to submit."

There is no artificial ceiling on:
- novelty,
- depth of understanding,
- theoretical or empirical rigor,
- scope of scientific insight,
- potential venue quality,
- potential impact on the broader AI field.

When evidence reveals that the project can support a deeper question, broader mechanism, stronger explanation, or more consequential contribution, allow the research to grow.

However:

> **Scientific ambition may be unbounded; scientific claims are bounded by evidence.**

Never use venue ambition to justify:
- overclaiming,
- unsupported generalization,
- inflated scope,
- unnecessary architectural complexity,
- hiding negative evidence,
- reverse-engineering a story to resemble a top-tier paper.

Venue targeting is downstream of:
**question quality → scientific depth → contribution strength → evidence quality → field significance → venue fit.**

A lower-ranked venue does not automatically imply that a work is "engineering," and a high-ranked venue does not automatically imply deep science. Scientific depth must be judged by the question, mechanism, evidence, contribution, and knowledge gained.

The default mindset is not:
> "What is enough to publish?"

It is:
> **"How far can this scientific question honestly go, and what is the strongest contribution we can truly defend?"**

## 3. The Canonical Operating Loop

The project state is not a single stage number. Research may occupy multiple states at once.

### Core loop

1. **Understand the problem**
2. **Map the literature**
3. **Deep-read key work**
4. **Synthesize across papers**
5. **Notice tensions / unanswered questions**
6. **Generate candidate explanations**
7. **Debate and attack them**
8. **Form a testable hypothesis**
9. **Design the cheapest informative experiment**
10. **Run / observe**
11. **Interpret conservatively**
12. **Update beliefs**
13. **Return to literature / question / idea / method as needed**
14. **Repeat until a bounded contribution emerges**

This is not linear. Evidence can force a return to any earlier point.

## 4. Canonical Subsystems

### A — Academic Knowledge & Deep Reading

Goal: reconstruct the intellectual structure of important papers.

For anchor papers, aim to recover:

> Problem/Context → Assumptions/Pressure → Observation → Insight → Mechanism → Evidence → Limitations

Levels of understanding:
- Recognition
- Functional understanding
- Mechanistic understanding
- Intellectual reconstruction
- Critical reconstruction
- Generative understanding

Important practices:
- adaptive reading,
- prerequisite expansion,
- author-intent reconstruction,
- architecture as information flow,
- equations as behavior/assumptions,
- experiments as arguments,
- closed-book reconstruction,
- paper interrogation,
- reverse defense.

#### Mastery check
Without notes, can the researcher:
- redraw the method,
- explain why every important component exists,
- identify the real novelty,
- identify strongest evidence,
- state limitations,
- connect it to the field?

---

### B — Scientific Question Formation

Goal: learn to ask better scientific questions.

Useful question operators:
- Why?
- Where in the pipeline?
- Under what conditions?
- What if an assumption is removed?
- What would happen under an oracle?
- Which factor drives the aggregate result?
- What boundary causes failure?
- What alternative explanation exists?
- How do we know?

A good research question should be:
- precise,
- consequential,
- discriminative,
- grounded,
- generative.

#### Mastery check
If answer A vs answer B would not change belief or action, reconsider whether the question is scientifically consequential.

---

### C — Continuous Scientific Debate

Goal: make adversarial reasoning a normal research activity.

Debate at:
- paper level,
- field level,
- idea level,
- experiment level,
- result level,
- contribution level.

Rotate roles:
- researcher,
- skeptic,
- reviewer,
- competing author,
- method expert,
- statistician,
- domain expert.

Professional debate concerns:
- claims,
- assumptions,
- evidence,
- interpretation.

Not ego.

---

### D — Literature Survey & Field Reconstruction

Goal: build a mental model of the field.

Key practices:
- breadth-first exploration before deep reading,
- backward citation chaining,
- forward citation chaining,
- conceptual search beyond terminology,
- find ancestor papers,
- identify turning points,
- inspect dead branches and negative results,
- build mechanistic taxonomies,
- periodically pause to synthesize,
- adversarially search against current beliefs,
- monitor concurrent work,
- check novelty continuously.

Survey saturation means:
- new papers mostly fit the existing field map,
- marginal information gain is low,
- the researcher can explain the field and its open tensions,
- closest work and unresolved questions are identifiable.

Survey never truly ends; it changes mode:
- exploratory,
- question-driven,
- hypothesis-driven,
- novelty verification,
- reviewer-defense.

---

### E — Research Questions & Idea Formation

Goal: move from field understanding to candidate contributions.

Do not start with:
> “What module can I add?”

Start with:
> “What do I still not understand?”

Look for:
- contradictions,
- repeated failure modes,
- hidden assumptions,
- mismatches,
- stage-localized failures,
- unexplained results,
- missing interactions,
- unused signals.

Idea maturity:
1. Spark
2. Question
3. Hypothesis
4. Candidate mechanism
5. Scientific proposal
6. Experimental candidate
7. Supported direction

Maintain competing explanations when reasonable.

Before coding, attack the idea:
- novelty,
- mechanism,
- alternative explanations,
- simpler interventions,
- falsification,
- closest prior work.

#### Mastery check
Without notes, explain:
- observation,
- why it matters,
- competing explanations,
- current hypothesis,
- proposed intervention,
- falsifier,
- closest work,
- strongest reviewer objection.

---

### F — Experimental Epistemology & Failure Loop

Goal: use experiments to learn, not just to improve scores.

Before important experiments:
- current belief,
- alternatives,
- uncertainty,
- predictions,
- controls,
- confounds,
- interpretation plan.

Prefer:
- minimal informative experiments,
- oracles,
- controlled interventions,
- negative controls,
- positive controls,
- mechanistic observables,
- bounded tuning.

Failure taxonomy:
- implementation failure,
- optimization failure,
- measurement failure,
- experimental-design failure,
- hypothesis failure,
- scope failure,
- interpretation failure.

After each substantive experiment ask:

> **What belief changed, and why?**

Do not silently mutate H1 into H2 while claiming H1 succeeded.

Distinguish:
- exploratory evidence,
- confirmatory evidence.

Use replication and multi-seed testing proportionally to:
- variance,
- effect size,
- claim strength,
- research stage.

---

### G — Scientific Claim, Contribution & Paper-worthiness

Goal: convert evidence into a bounded scientific contribution.

Distinguish:
- observation,
- finding,
- claim,
- contribution.

Possible contributions:
- phenomenon,
- explanation,
- method,
- representation,
- objective,
- theory,
- evaluation,
- dataset,
- meaningful negative result.

Use the knowledge-contribution diagnostic:

> Before this work, the field did not know / could not reliably do X.\
> Our work shows Y, explains or enables it through Z, supported by evidence E.

Ask repeatedly:

> **So what?**

A contribution should change how the field:
- understands,
- designs,
- evaluates,
- or reasons.

Build a claim–evidence graph.

Confront closest work directly.

Search for simpler explanations.

State failure boundaries.

Run mock rejection and mock acceptance exercises.

---

### H — Paper Construction as Scientific Argument

Goal: write a paper as a coherent scientific argument, not a collection of sections.

Canonical logical story:

> Problem/Context → Unresolved limitation/tension → Observation/Question → Insight/Hypothesis → Method derived from insight → Ordered evidence → Implications → Limitations

Important principles:
- title is a scoped claim,
- abstract is compressed argument,
- introduction creates intellectual necessity,
- related work synthesizes and positions fairly,
- method explains why the design follows from the hypothesis,
- equations carry meaning,
- figures and tables carry argumentative roles,
- result order follows logic, not research chronology,
- limitations are real,
- discussion explains what the field learns,
- claim language matches evidence.

Writing may reveal missing research. That is useful.

Do not fabricate a clean historical chronology if the idea emerged later; distinguish logical presentation from false hindsight.

---

### I — Reviewer Simulation, Rebuttal & Scientific Defense

Goal: make the research defensible before external review.

Reviewer personas:
- novelty,
- methodology,
- empirical,
- theory/mechanism,
- domain,
- reproducibility,
- skeptical generalist.

Objection severity:
- cosmetic,
- repairable,
- structural,
- fatal.

Practices:
- strongest-possible reviewer,
- mock rejection,
- objection trees,
- evidence-first rebuttal,
- intelligent concession,
- word-limit prioritization,
- reviewer disagreement analysis,
- meta-review simulation,
- 30-second defense,
- hostile-question drills,
- whiteboard defense,
- oral Q&A,
- uncertainty-aware answers.

Important:
- Reviewer is not the enemy.
- Accept is not truth.
- Reject is not necessarily research failure.
- Every review cycle produces evidence about the paper.

---

### J — Researcher Development & Long-Term Knowledge

Goal: make research capability compound across projects.

Preserve lightly:
- field map,
- concept map,
- anchor papers,
- open questions,
- belief history,
- idea notes,
- evidence ledger,
- dead ends,
- reviewer lessons,
- postmortem.

Do not create a “second brain bureaucracy.”

Mental model > notes.

Periodically:
- reconstruct the field from memory,
- re-synthesize the field,
- review unresolved questions,
- revisit theory gaps,
- study excellent papers for research taste,
- study weak papers for critical judgment,
- practice debate independently,
- transfer reusable concepts across projects.

#### End-of-project postmortem
Review:
- intellectual change,
- methodological lessons,
- failed ideas,
- most informative experiments,
- literature lessons,
- reviewer lessons,
- wasted effort,
- skills improved,
- open questions,
- next research directions.

## 5. Research State Model

Do not track only “current phase.”

Track at least:

### Knowledge state
What do we understand?

### Question state
What are the most important unresolved questions?

### Belief state
What explanations do we currently believe, and with what confidence?

### Evidence state
What observations support or contradict those beliefs?

### Idea state
What candidate explanations or methods exist?

### Dead-end state
What has already been rejected, disproven, or abandoned?

Research can occupy several of these states simultaneously.

## 6. Minimal Durable Artifacts

Use only what materially helps reasoning.

Recommended minimal set:

- `field_map`
- `anchor_papers`
- `question_map`
- `belief_history`
- `idea_notes`
- `evidence_ledger`
- `dead_ends`
- `paper_story`
- `postmortem`

These are conceptual artifacts, not mandatory filenames.

Do not create more structure unless the research complexity justifies it.

---

## 7. Tool Selection Policy

Choose tools by cognitive bottleneck.

### Need to remember / reason in prose
Use Markdown / plain notes / reference manager.

### Need to compare many items
Use spreadsheet.

### Need to see structure or information flow
Use whiteboard / diagram / draw.io / Figma.

### Need to communicate or compress a story
Use slides.

### Need to analyze evidence
Use notebook / script.

### Need reproducibility
Use code, versioning, configs, logs.

### Need experiment tracking
Use a tracker only if run volume genuinely warrants it.

Rule:

> **If the tool does not reduce friction, reveal structure, improve evidence handling, or improve communication, do not use it.**

## 8. Professional Reasoning Standard

For every major research decision, be able to answer:

1. What do we know?
2. What do we not know?
3. What evidence supports the current belief?
4. What evidence contradicts it?
5. What alternative explanations exist?
6. What assumptions are we making?
7. How uncertain are we?
8. What would falsify the current belief?
9. What is the most scientifically informative next action?
10. Why is that action better than plausible alternatives?

This reasoning can be implicit for small decisions, but major decisions must meet this standard.

## 9. Human Mastery Checks

The researcher should increasingly be able to:

### Paper mastery
- reconstruct anchor papers from memory,
- explain architecture and equations mechanistically,
- critique evidence,
- defend or reject claims.

### Field mastery
- draw the field map,
- explain lineage and paradigms,
- identify assumptions and tensions,
- place a new paper into the landscape.

### Idea mastery
- explain why an idea matters,
- state competing hypotheses,
- predict outcomes,
- identify falsifiers.

### Experiment mastery
- justify controls,
- explain what a result does and does not show,
- update beliefs honestly.

### Contribution mastery
- state what the field learns,
- defend novelty,
- map claims to evidence,
- state boundaries.

### Defense mastery
- survive skeptical questioning,
- answer without scripts,
- distinguish known / believed / hypothesized / unknown,
- concede valid criticism,
- say “I do not know yet” when appropriate.

## 10. Canonical Research Progress Criterion

Do **not** measure progress primarily by:
- paper count,
- number of runs,
- number of notes,
- number of papers read,
- benchmark score,
- artifacts produced.

A research step is valuable if it improves at least one of:

- the mental model,
- research taste,
- question quality,
- critical ability,
- generative ability,
- evidence quality,
- confidence calibration,
- scientific claim quality.

---

## 11. Canonical Start Rule for Future Pure Research Projects

When a future project is clearly pure academic research:

1. Automatically use this framework.
2. Do not substitute challenge/production stage-gates.
3. Start by reconstructing the problem and academic context.
4. Identify what the researcher must learn deeply.
5. Build the field map and anchor-paper set.
6. Establish active questions, beliefs, and uncertainties.
7. Use debate and closed-book reconstruction continuously.
8. Let ideas emerge from tensions and understanding.
9. Formalize only mature ideas.
10. Prefer the cheapest informative experiment.
11. Treat failure as evidence.
12. Loop backward whenever evidence demands it.
13. Crystallize claims only when claim–evidence chains are coherent.
14. Treat writing/review/defense as part of research.
15. Preserve researcher growth and scientific memory after the project.

## 13. One-Sentence Identity

> **Pure academic research is not a pipeline that converts papers into a method; it is an iterative scientific process that transforms the researcher into someone capable of discovering, testing, defending, and communicating a genuine contribution to the field.**
